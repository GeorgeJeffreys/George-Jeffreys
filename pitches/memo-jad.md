# Memo for Jad — the two strategies worth your time

*From the Phase 0 strategy scout, 14 September 2026. Six independent lanes were researched and then attacked in a red-team pass. Four are not worth your attention: an XBRL fundamentals tilt whose own author declared the lane dead, S&P index deletions that turn out to be a small-cap value exposure wearing a costume, federal contract award surprises whose kill threshold would pass a strategy that cannot pay its own costs, and defined-risk earnings straddle selling, which is the only genuinely uncorrelated idea but carries 200 basis points of commission and a compliance profile that excludes you entirely. These two are the survivors, written by the researchers who did the work. Neither beats the Disclosure Change Engine on expected Sharpe. Both beat it on build time, capital efficiency and how cheaply they can be killed. Two decisions are asked of you at the end of each section, and one of them is about your career, not the trade.*

## Small-cap cash merger arbitrage

**The trade.** When a US-listed company worth $75m to $1bn agrees to be bought for cash, its shares sit a few percent below the agreed price until closing, and we buy that gap and hold it to the cash-out. Long only, fifteen to twenty positions, terms taken from the merger agreement filed on EDGAR.

**Why the money is there.** The spread pays for the risk the deal breaks and for locking capital up for four months. Both are priced efficiently in large deals because thirty arbitrage desks look at every one. In a $300m target they do not look, because a fund running $2bn cannot put a meaningful ticket into a name trading $500k a day without moving it. Jetley and Ji showed spreads compressed by over 400bp after 2002 as money flowed into merger arb; the claim here is that the money went where it could be sized, and small deals were left alone. The uncomfortable counterpoint is that Baker and Savaşoglu, looking at 1981 to 1996, found the opposite — returns rose with target size. I think capital abundance has flipped the sign since; I cannot prove it from the literature, and it is what the kill test measures.

**What it realistically pays, and why not more.** Four to seven percent over T-bills, volatility of five to eight percent, so a Sharpe of 0.5 to 1.0. That is the same range as the *Lazy Prices* project, not better. The case for it rests on three things: six weeks to build rather than six months, about ten basis points a year in commission, and a few days on WRDS that can kill it outright. One soft number matters: I estimate sixty to a hundred and twenty deals a year pass the filters. If it is nearer thirty, the book sits half in cash and the Sharpe halves with it.

**The tail.** The return profile is a sold index put. It earns steadily and loses badly all at once, because deals break together when credit closes — 2008, March 2020. A single break costs twenty to forty percent of that position as the stock falls back to its pre-bid price, and a fifteen to twenty percent drawdown in a bad quarter is entirely plausible. There is no hedge; diversification is the only defence and it fails exactly when needed.

**Your problem, not George's.** This lane is the worst possible fit for an incoming banker. The strategy systematically trades announced deals, which is exactly the population your firm advises on and puts on its restricted list. Most banks require pre-clearance of every individual-name trade, a thirty-day minimum hold and disclosure of every account; Goldman has barred bankers from individual stocks, and from event-driven funds, since 2014. FINRA Rule 3210 requires your employer's prior written consent for any account in which you hold a beneficial interest, and presumes you have one in a spouse's accounts too. A book that must buy within days of announcement cannot run through a pre-clearance queue, and you cannot ask why a name is blocked without learning something you should not know. My honest read: from your start date you can have no beneficial interest and no deal-specific input. George owns the capital and the trades; you contribute design judgement now and stop.

**The decision.** Are you content for George to own this book outright, with you as an unpaid adviser before your start date and nothing after it? If yes, three to five days of WRDS work tells us whether the small-deal spread survives breaks. If no, we stop here and put the six weeks into *Lazy Prices*.


## Clustered insider purchases

**The trade.** When two or more officers or directors of the same US small cap ($75m–$2bn) buy their own stock on the open market within ten trading days of each other, we buy at the next close after the second Form 4 lands and hold for a fixed period. Long-only in single names, because insider selling predicts nothing, and hedged with a Russell 2000 short.

**Why the money is there.** Not that it is undiscovered — free websites screen for it — but that it cannot be competed away, because return is inversely proportional to size. Cziraki and Gider find returns to insider purchases falling with both trade and firm size: the money sits in $200m companies where the insider risks $40k of his own. No fund running $2bn can take a meaningful position in a stock trading $400k a day, and nobody with the engineering to automate this has a mandate permitting $5,000 tickets. Note what that concedes: above about $2m the filters strip the names carrying the signal. This is an edge for the money we have, never a business. The fair attack is that capacity is the standard alibi for an anomaly that was never there.

**What it pays, and why the pitch overstates it.** The pitch claims 2–3% per event over sixty days, 8–12% gross, Sharpe 0.6. Its own evidence does not support that. Alldredge and Blank find roughly 2% in the first month; Zhao's 2026 microcap study finds confidence intervals containing zero by day 63. Whatever exists is spent inside a month; the back half of the hold takes risk for nothing. Match the hold to the evidence — twenty days — and you triple the order count and roughly triple the 350–400bp cost drag. The long version overstates the gross; the short pays much of it back in spread. So 0.6 is the ceiling, not the centre; zero is inside the range. A further claim, that 70–80% of the return accrues before the filing where nobody can trade, rests on an uncited Substack post and is not part of the case.

**What it correlates with.** Residual risk is small-cap value and reversal — we buy beaten-down microcaps. It draws down with them, 20–25% in a 2020-style liquidation, and that is when clusters spike, so the signal fires hardest into the fall. Three of the six lanes share this shape, long small caps against a Russell short. Running two of them is one bet, sized twice.

**The compliance position.** Better than merger arbitrage, but not free. We trade $200m companies your firm will not advise and will rarely restrict, on public information. But banks pre-clear every trade, and 200–250 orders a year will test that queue; they impose thirty-day minimum holds; and FINRA Rule 3210 requires your employer's written consent for any account in which you hold a beneficial interest, a spouse's included. Plainly: a sixty-day hold clears a thirty-day minimum, a twenty-day hold does not. The version the evidence supports is the one you cannot trade; the one you can trade is the one I have just told you is overstated. Either you hold an interest and we run sixty days with the weaker case, or George runs twenty days and you own none of it.

**The decision.** Do we spend three days trying to kill this before committing six weeks to build it? The test rebuilds cluster events from SEC insider data, 2015–2025, and measures hedged returns at day 21 and day 63. Two to three days of George's time, no capital: the cheapest no available, on the lane ranked second of six.

## What is actually being asked of you

**Two kill tests, eight days of George's time, no capital.** Both strategies can be falsified on Wharton data before a line of production code is written. Merger arbitrage costs three to five days and answers whether small-deal spreads survive their own break rate. Insider clusters cost two to three days and answers whether anything survives past day twenty-one. Run both, or run neither, but do not build first.

**One question that is yours alone.** Both sections land in the same place from different directions: your beneficial interest is the binding constraint, not the data, not the engineering and not the capital. Merger arbitrage trades exactly the deal population your firm restricts, so from your start date you can hold no interest and give no deal-specific input. Insider clusters are cleaner, but the sixty-day version that clears a thirty-day minimum hold is the version the evidence does not support, and the twenty-day version the evidence does support is the one you cannot trade. Settle this with your firm's compliance desk before either kill test runs. It is cheaper than both tests combined and it determines which versions are even on the table.
