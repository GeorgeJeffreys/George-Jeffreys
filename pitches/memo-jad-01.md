## Clustered insider purchases

**The trade.** When two or more officers or directors of the same US small cap ($75m–$2bn) buy their own stock on the open market within ten trading days of each other, we buy at the next close after the second Form 4 lands and hold for a fixed period. Long-only in single names, because insider selling predicts nothing, and hedged with a Russell 2000 short.

**Why the money is there.** Not that it is undiscovered — free websites screen for it — but that it cannot be competed away, because return is inversely proportional to size. Cziraki and Gider find returns to insider purchases falling with both trade and firm size: the money sits in $200m companies where the insider risks $40k of his own. No fund running $2bn can take a meaningful position in a stock trading $400k a day, and nobody with the engineering to automate this has a mandate permitting $5,000 tickets. Note what that concedes: above about $2m the filters strip the names carrying the signal. This is an edge for the money we have, never a business. The fair attack is that capacity is the standard alibi for an anomaly that was never there.

**What it pays, and why the pitch overstates it.** The pitch claims 2–3% per event over sixty days, 8–12% gross, Sharpe 0.6. Its own evidence does not support that. Alldredge and Blank find roughly 2% in the first month; Zhao's 2026 microcap study finds confidence intervals containing zero by day 63. Whatever exists is spent inside a month; the back half of the hold takes risk for nothing. Match the hold to the evidence — twenty days — and you triple the order count and roughly triple the 350–400bp cost drag. The long version overstates the gross; the short pays much of it back in spread. So 0.6 is the ceiling, not the centre; zero is inside the range. A further claim, that 70–80% of the return accrues before the filing where nobody can trade, rests on an uncited Substack post and is not part of the case.

**What it correlates with.** Residual risk is small-cap value and reversal — we buy beaten-down microcaps. It draws down with them, 20–25% in a 2020-style liquidation, and that is when clusters spike, so the signal fires hardest into the fall. Three of the six lanes share this shape, long small caps against a Russell short. Running two of them is one bet, sized twice.

**The compliance position.** Better than merger arbitrage, but not free. We trade $200m companies your firm will not advise and will rarely restrict, on public information. But banks pre-clear every trade, and 200–250 orders a year will test that queue; they impose thirty-day minimum holds; and FINRA Rule 3210 requires your employer's written consent for any account in which you hold a beneficial interest, a spouse's included. Plainly: a sixty-day hold clears a thirty-day minimum, a twenty-day hold does not. The version the evidence supports is the one you cannot trade; the one you can trade is the one I have just told you is overstated. Either you hold an interest and we run sixty days with the weaker case, or George runs twenty days and you own none of it.

**The decision.** Do we spend three days trying to kill this before committing six weeks to build it? The test rebuilds cluster events from SEC insider data, 2015–2025, and measures hedged returns at day 21 and day 63. Two to three days of George's time, no capital: the cheapest no available, on the lane ranked second of six.
