## Small-cap cash merger arbitrage

**The trade.** When a US-listed company worth $75m to $1bn agrees to be bought for cash, its shares sit a few percent below the agreed price until closing, and we buy that gap and hold it to the cash-out. Long only, fifteen to twenty positions, terms taken from the merger agreement filed on EDGAR.

**Why the money is there.** The spread pays for the risk the deal breaks and for locking capital up for four months. Both are priced efficiently in large deals because thirty arbitrage desks look at every one. In a $300m target they do not look, because a fund running $2bn cannot put a meaningful ticket into a name trading $500k a day without moving it. Jetley and Ji showed spreads compressed by over 400bp after 2002 as money flowed into merger arb; the claim here is that the money went where it could be sized, and small deals were left alone. The uncomfortable counterpoint is that Baker and Savaşoglu, looking at 1981 to 1996, found the opposite — returns rose with target size. I think capital abundance has flipped the sign since; I cannot prove it from the literature, and it is what the kill test measures.

**What it realistically pays, and why not more.** Four to seven percent over T-bills, volatility of five to eight percent, so a Sharpe of 0.5 to 1.0. That is the same range as the *Lazy Prices* project, not better. The case for it rests on three things: six weeks to build rather than six months, about ten basis points a year in commission, and a few days on WRDS that can kill it outright. One soft number matters: I estimate sixty to a hundred and twenty deals a year pass the filters. If it is nearer thirty, the book sits half in cash and the Sharpe halves with it.

**The tail.** The return profile is a sold index put. It earns steadily and loses badly all at once, because deals break together when credit closes — 2008, March 2020. A single break costs twenty to forty percent of that position as the stock falls back to its pre-bid price, and a fifteen to twenty percent drawdown in a bad quarter is entirely plausible. There is no hedge; diversification is the only defence and it fails exactly when needed.

**Your problem, not George's.** This lane is the worst possible fit for an incoming banker. The strategy systematically trades announced deals, which is exactly the population your firm advises on and puts on its restricted list. Most banks require pre-clearance of every individual-name trade, a thirty-day minimum hold and disclosure of every account; Goldman has barred bankers from individual stocks, and from event-driven funds, since 2014. FINRA Rule 3210 requires your employer's prior written consent for any account in which you hold a beneficial interest, and presumes you have one in a spouse's accounts too. A book that must buy within days of announcement cannot run through a pre-clearance queue, and you cannot ask why a name is blocked without learning something you should not know. My honest read: from your start date you can have no beneficial interest and no deal-specific input. George owns the capital and the trades; you contribute design judgement now and stop.

**The decision.** Are you content for George to own this book outright, with you as an unpaid adviser before your start date and nothing after it? If yes, three to five days of WRDS work tells us whether the small-deal spread survives breaks. If no, we stop here and put the six weeks into *Lazy Prices*.
